---
title: 'System Design: Proximity Service'
---

A proximity service answers one question at enormous scale: **what is near this point?** Review and discovery apps use it to list restaurants within a kilometer, ride-hailing apps to find nearby drivers, and dating or social apps to find people close by. In this chapter we design a Yelp-like service for discovering businesses near a user, and we examine the spatial indexing techniques that make "nearby" queries fast.

## Why nearby search is not trivial

Suppose each business is stored with its latitude and longitude. The obvious query is:

```sql
SELECT * FROM business
WHERE lat BETWEEN :lat - :d AND :lat + :d
  AND lng BETWEEN :lng - :d AND :lng + :d;
```

With an index on `lat` and another on `lng`, the database can use only one index efficiently. It finds all businesses in a thin horizontal band around the world, then filters them by longitude. With hundreds of millions of businesses, that band contains far too many rows. A composite index on (lat, lng) has the same problem, because a B-tree orders data along one dimension at a time.

The underlying issue is that **two-dimensional proximity does not map naturally onto one-dimensional indexes**. The solutions in this chapter, such as geohashes, quadtrees and cell-based systems like S2 or H3, all map 2D space into structures that keep nearby points together.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  Q["Query: businesses within 1 km"] --> Idx["Spatial index: which cells overlap the circle?"]
  Idx --> Cells["A handful of cells"]
  Cells --> Cand["Candidate businesses"]
  Cand --> Filter["Exact distance filter and ranking"]
  Filter --> R["Results"]
```

## Characteristics of the workload

- **Read-heavy.** Users search far more often than businesses are added or edited. A new business appearing in search results a few minutes later is fine.
- **Static data.** Businesses rarely move. This makes it practical to build spatial indexes offline and keep them in memory. (Moving objects, such as drivers, are a different problem that we tackle in the ride-hailing chapter.)
- **Uneven density.** A square kilometer of a city center may contain thousands of businesses, while a square kilometer of countryside contains none. A good index adapts to density.
- **Latency-sensitive.** Results must appear as the user pans the map or changes filters.

<Callout type="interview">
When asked to design a proximity service, get to the indexing question quickly. Interviewers want to hear why a plain latitude and longitude index fails and how a geohash or quadtree solves it.
</Callout>

<Ponder question="Why is 'find nearby drivers' a harder variant of the proximity problem than 'find nearby restaurants'?">
Restaurants almost never move, so their index can be built offline and refreshed every few minutes. Drivers move constantly and report their position every few seconds, so the index must absorb hundreds of thousands of location updates per second while staying queryable, and a result that is even 30 seconds stale may be wrong. That shifts the design towards in-memory, frequently updated indexes sharded by region — the approach in the ride-hailing chapter.
</Ponder>

<KeyTakeaways>
- A proximity service answers "what is near this point?" for discovery, ride-hailing and social apps.
- Separate B-tree indexes on latitude and longitude cannot efficiently answer 2D range queries.
- Spatial indexes such as geohashes, quadtrees, S2 and H3 map 2D space into structures that keep nearby points together.
- Business data is read-heavy and mostly static, so spatial indexes can be built offline and held in memory.
- Density varies enormously between cities and rural areas, so the index should adapt to it.
</KeyTakeaways>
