---
title: Requirements of a Maps Service
---

This lesson defines what the maps service must do and estimates how much load it must carry.

## Functional requirements

- **View the map:** users pan and zoom a map of the world on web and mobile.
- **Find places:** users search for addresses and points of interest, and see where they are on the map.
- **Get directions:** given a start and destination, the service returns one or more routes for driving, walking or cycling, with distance and estimated time of arrival (ETA).
- **Navigate:** during a trip the app gives turn-by-turn directions, tracks the user's position and reroutes if they leave the route or if a faster route appears.
- **Live traffic:** routes and ETAs account for current traffic conditions.

Out of scope: public transit schedules, street-level imagery, offline map downloads, reviews and business listings management.

## Non-functional requirements

- **Low latency:** tiles should appear within a couple of hundred milliseconds; a route should be returned in about a second; rerouting during navigation should feel immediate.
- **High availability:** navigation is often used while driving, so the service must be dependable. The client should keep guiding the user along the current route even if the network drops.
- **Accuracy:** ETAs should be close to actual travel times, and routes should respect turn restrictions and one-way streets.
- **Scalability:** the system must handle hundreds of millions of daily users and rush-hour peaks.
- **Freshness:** traffic data should reflect conditions within a few minutes; road network changes can take hours or days.

## Capacity estimation

| Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Daily active users | 1 billion |
| Route requests per active user per day | 2 |
| Concurrent navigating devices at peak | 50 million |
| Location ping interval while navigating | every 5 seconds |
| Tile requests per session | 100, mostly served by CDN |
| Road graph size | about 500 million nodes and 1 billion edges |

**Route requests:** 1 billion × 2 = 2 billion per day, about 23,000 per second on average and perhaps 70,000 per second at rush-hour peaks. Navigation adds reroute requests on top.

**Location pings:** 50 million devices ÷ 5 seconds = 10 million pings per second at peak. Each ping is small, around 100 bytes with position, speed, heading and timestamp, so this is about 1 GB per second of ingest, or roughly 8 Gbps. Pings are batched on the device to cut request overhead.

**Tiles:** 1 billion users × 100 tiles = 100 billion tile requests per day, over a million per second. Almost all are served from CDN caches because popular areas are requested repeatedly.

**Map data storage:** the road graph with attributes is on the order of a few hundred gigabytes. Vector tiles for all zoom levels are tens of terabytes. Satellite imagery, if included, is petabytes.

**Traffic history:** storing aggregated speeds per road segment every few minutes for a billion edges produces large but manageable volumes. Historical data powers ETA predictions for future departure times.

<Callout type="note">
The estimates show three very different workloads: a massive, cacheable read workload for tiles; a CPU-intensive, latency-sensitive workload for routing; and a write-heavy streaming workload for location pings. Each gets its own design.
</Callout>

## Server estimation

If one routing server with an in-memory, preprocessed graph answers about 500 queries per second, the 70,000-per-second peak needs about 140 servers, plus redundancy. Each routing server must hold the preprocessed graph for its region in memory, which drives the choice of large-memory machines and regional partitioning. Ingesting 10 million pings per second requires a partitioned streaming platform with hundreds of partitions.

## How many tiles are there?

Each zoom level has `4^z` tiles: 1 at zoom 0, about 1 million at zoom 10, about 69 billion at zoom 18. Most of the planet is ocean or empty land, and many high-zoom tiles are never viewed, so tiles are generated for populated areas and on demand elsewhere. Vector tiles also shrink storage because one tile's geometry can be rendered at several display sizes and styles on the client.

| Zoom | Tiles worldwide | Typical view |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | Whole world |
| 5 | ~1,000 | Continent |
| 10 | ~1 million | Metropolitan area |
| 15 | ~1 billion | Streets |
| 18 | ~69 billion | Buildings |

<Ponder question="Ten million location pings per second sounds enormous. Could the service simply sample fewer pings?">
For traffic estimation, yes to a degree: a busy highway segment gets far more pings than needed, so pings can be sampled or aggregated on the device. But navigation for each user still needs frequent position updates to detect missed turns quickly, and quiet roads need every ping they can get. A practical design batches pings on the device (fewer requests), keeps full resolution for the user's own navigation, and down-samples only where coverage is already dense.
</Ponder>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Core features are map viewing, place search, directions, turn-by-turn navigation and live traffic.
- Latency, availability and ETA accuracy are the most important non-functional requirements.
- Tiles are a huge but cacheable read load; routing is CPU- and memory-intensive; location pings are a heavy streaming write load.
- Estimates suggest tens of thousands of route requests and around 10 million location pings per second at peak.
- Tile counts grow as 4^z, so high-zoom tiles are generated for populated areas and on demand.
</KeyTakeaways>
