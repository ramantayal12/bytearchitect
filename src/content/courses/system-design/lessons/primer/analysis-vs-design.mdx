---
title: System Analysis versus System Design
---

"System analysis" and "system design" are often mentioned together, and some job descriptions and interviews use one term when they mean the other. They are related but distinct activities. Understanding the difference helps you recognize what an interviewer or project is really asking for.

## System analysis: understanding the problem

System analysis studies an existing situation or a proposed need to understand **what** a system must do. It focuses on the problem, not the solution. Typical activities include:

- Interviewing stakeholders and users to gather requirements.
- Studying current processes and systems, and their shortcomings.
- Modeling data, workflows and business rules.
- Identifying constraints, such as regulations, budgets, timelines and existing infrastructure.
- Assessing feasibility: technical, economic and operational.
- Producing a requirements specification that everyone agrees on.

The output answers questions like "Which problems are we solving? For whom? What must be true for this to succeed?"

## System design: shaping the solution

System design takes the requirements and decides **how** the system will meet them. It focuses on the solution:

- Choosing an architecture and its components.
- Defining interfaces, APIs and data models.
- Selecting technologies and storage.
- Planning for scalability, availability, security and cost.
- Evaluating trade-offs and failure modes.
- Producing architecture diagrams and design documents that engineers implement.

## Comparing them

| Aspect | System analysis | System design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central question | What is needed and why? | How will we build it? |
| Focus | Problem domain, users, processes | Architecture, components, technologies |
| Key outputs | Requirements, process and data models, feasibility | Architecture, APIs, data models, design documents |
| Typical roles | Business or systems analysts, product managers | Architects, senior engineers |
| Success measure | The right problem is understood | The solution meets requirements within constraints |

## Where they meet in interviews

A system design interview contains a small amount of analysis at the start. The **Scope** step of SCALED is analysis: clarifying users, features and constraints. The rest is design. Candidates who skip the analysis step often design an impressive system for the wrong problem.

In real projects, the two alternate. Designing reveals new questions (can we accept eventual consistency here?), which send you back to analysis (what does the business actually need?). Mature teams treat requirements and designs as living documents that evolve together.

## Practical takeaways

- Start every design with enough analysis to know what matters and what does not.
- Separate facts (requirements, constraints) from choices (architecture, technology) in documents and discussions.
- When a design debate stalls, it is often because the requirements are unclear; return to analysis.

## Analysis questions that change designs

| Analysis question | Why the answer changes the design |
| --- | --- |
| Can users tolerate seeing slightly stale data? | Decides caching and consistency models |
| What happens if we lose the last few seconds of writes? | Decides synchronous versus asynchronous replication |
| Where are users, and are there data residency rules? | Decides regions and data placement |
| How spiky is traffic, and is it predictable? | Decides pre-scaling, queues and admission control |
| Who operates this, and how large is the team? | Decides managed services versus self-hosting, monolith versus services |

<Ponder question="Two senior engineers argue for an hour about whether to use a relational database or a key-value store. What analysis step might resolve it?">
Clarifying the access patterns and guarantees the product needs: which queries must be fast, whether multi-row transactions are required, expected write volume and growth, and whether ad-hoc querying matters. The argument usually stalls because each engineer is assuming different requirements; writing them down often makes the choice obvious.
</Ponder>

<KeyTakeaways>
- System analysis determines what a system must do and why; system design determines how it will do it.
- Analysis produces requirements, models and feasibility assessments; design produces architecture, interfaces and design documents.
- In interviews, the Scope step is analysis, and the remaining SCALED steps are design.
- In practice the two alternate, and unclear requirements are a common cause of stalled design debates.
</KeyTakeaways>
