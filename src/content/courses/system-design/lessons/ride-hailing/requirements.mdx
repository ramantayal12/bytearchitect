---
title: Requirements of a Ride-Hailing Service
---

This lesson defines the scope of the ride-hailing service and estimates its load.

## Functional requirements

**Riders can:**

- see nearby available drivers on a map before requesting;
- request a ride from a pickup to a destination and see an upfront price estimate;
- be matched with a driver, see the driver's details and watch the car approach;
- cancel a ride, pay automatically at the end and rate the driver.

**Drivers can:**

- go online and offline and share their location while online;
- receive trip offers and accept or decline them within a few seconds;
- navigate to pickup and destination, and see their earnings.

Out of scope: shared or pooled rides, scheduled rides, driver onboarding and background checks, and customer support tooling.

## Non-functional requirements

- **Low latency:** a rider should be matched within a few seconds; location updates should appear on the other party's map within a second or two.
- **High availability:** an outage strands riders and costs drivers income. The request and matching path must be highly available.
- **Consistency where it matters:** a driver must never be assigned to two trips, and payments must be exactly right. Elsewhere, such as the nearby-cars map, eventual consistency is fine.
- **Scalability:** the service must handle city-level peaks, such as after concerts or during storms.
- **Reliability of payments:** no double charges and no lost payments, even under retries and failures.

## Capacity estimation

| Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly active riders | 100 million |
| Daily trips | 20 million |
| Drivers online at peak | 3 million |
| Driver location update interval | every 4 seconds |
| Riders with the app open at peak | 10 million, each polling nearby cars |

**Location updates:** 3 million drivers ÷ 4 seconds = 750,000 updates per second at peak. Each update carries driver ID, coordinates, heading, speed and timestamp, about 100 bytes, so roughly 75 MB per second of ingest.

**Trip requests:** 20 million trips per day is about 230 per second on average. Peaks can be five times higher, so plan for over 1,000 requests per second, each triggering a nearby-driver search and several offers.

**Nearby-car views:** if riders with the app open refresh nearby cars every 5 seconds, 10 million ÷ 5 = 2 million queries per second at peak. This read path does not need perfect freshness and can be served from an approximate, cached view per area.

**Storage:** trip records of about 2 KB each, 20 million per day, is 40 GB per day, roughly 15 TB per year. Location history for completed trips, such as a sampled route polyline, adds more but can be compressed and moved to cold storage.

<Callout type="note">
Notice the asymmetry: trip requests are only hundreds to thousands per second, but location updates are hundreds of thousands per second and nearby-car views millions. The hard scaling problem is locations, not trips.
</Callout>

## Bandwidth and connections

With a few million drivers and riders connected at peak, the service maintains millions of long-lived connections, such as WebSockets or MQTT sessions, to push offers and updates. These are spread across a fleet of connection gateway servers, each holding perhaps 50,000 to 100,000 connections.

## Geographic partitioning

Trips are local: a rider in one city is never matched with a driver in another. This makes **city or region** a natural partition key for location data and matching, which limits the blast radius of failures and lets each region scale independently.

<Ponder question="Why not store every driver location update as a row in a relational database and query it with a spatial index?">
At 750,000 updates per second, each an index-updating write, the database would be overwhelmed, and almost all of those rows are obsolete within seconds — only the latest position per driver matters for matching. Durable writes buy nothing for data that will be replaced four seconds later. An in-memory index holding the latest position per driver, rebuilt from the update stream after a crash, fits the data's ephemeral nature.
</Ponder>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Riders request, track and pay for trips; drivers share location and accept offers.
- Matching must be fast and must never double-assign a driver; payments must be exact.
- Location updates reach hundreds of thousands per second, and nearby-car views millions, far more than trip requests.
- Millions of long-lived connections are needed to push offers and live locations.
- Trips are local, so city or region is a natural partition key for locations and matching.
</KeyTakeaways>
