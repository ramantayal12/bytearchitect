## Approach: place the tallest people first

The key observation: a person's `k` only counts people who are **at least as tall** as them. Shorter people are invisible to them.

So place people from tallest to shortest. When it's someone's turn, everyone already in the queue is at least as tall, so inserting them at index `k` puts exactly `k` of those people in front. Everyone inserted afterwards is shorter (or equally tall, but sorted after, see below), so they never change this person's count, wherever they go.

Among people of **equal** height, insert the one with the smaller `k` first. The one with the larger `k` must end up behind it, and inserting it later at its larger index does exactly that.

1. Sort by height descending, then by `k` ascending.
2. Insert each person at index `k` of the result.

## Complexity

- **Time:** `O(n²)` in the worst case, because each insertion shifts up to `n` elements. For `n <= 2000` that's about two million moves, which is fast.
- **Space:** `O(n)` for the result.

## About the follow-up

Reverse the idea: place people from **shortest** to tallest into an array of `n` empty slots. A person with key `[h, k]` goes into the `(k + 1)`-th empty slot, counting from the front. Slots left empty will later hold taller people, which the person must count. Ties need care: with equal heights, process the larger `k` first. A Fenwick tree (binary indexed tree) over the slots finds the `(k + 1)`-th empty slot in `O(log n)`, for `O(n log n)` overall.
